# Overview of the families

372 families across 17 disciplines. Each entry gives the question addressed, the main result, and the manuscripts in the family.

## Metaphysics (MET)

**MET-01 — Why is there something rather than nothing?** Because the empty world is not a stable possibility. Under plausible principles of modal plenitude, the empty world receives measure zero among the possibilities. Formalized. Manuscripts: MET-01-a, MET-01-b, MET-01-c, MET-01-d, MET-01-e.

**MET-02 — Do universals exist?** Yes. A sparse theory of universals is defended: there are few genuine universals, and all other properties are constructed from them. Formalized. Manuscripts: MET-02-a, MET-02-b.

**MET-03 — Does the external world exist?** Yes. Idealism is consistent but requires one more primitive than realism, and is eliminated by parsimony. Formalized. Manuscripts: MET-03-a, MET-03-b.

**MET-04 — What is a cause?** A cause is a counterfactual difference-maker selected by norms of normality. Causation is dependence plus default. Formalized. Manuscripts: MET-04-a, MET-04-b, MET-04-c.

**MET-05 — How many clouds are in the sky? (The problem of the many)** One. The many candidate aggregates are not each a cloud; the vagueness of "cloud" selects one by supervaluation. Manuscripts: MET-05-a, MET-05-b.

**MET-06 — When do several things compose one?** Exactly when the parts share a common fate under the relevant dynamics. Tables and organisms exist; arbitrary scattered sums do not. Formalized. Manuscripts: MET-06-a, MET-06-b, MET-06-c.

**MET-07 — Which ship is the Ship of Theseus?** The continuously repaired ship. The reassembled ship is a different ship composed of the original planks. Manuscripts: MET-07-a, MET-07-b.

**MET-08 — Are there tropes?** No. Tropes are best understood as particularized universals, and trope theory adds nothing that a theory of universals lacks. Manuscripts: MET-08-a.

**MET-09 — Is reality fundamentally one?** Yes. The cosmos is the only fundamental object; ordinary objects are abstractions from it. Manuscripts: MET-09-a, MET-09-b.

**MET-10 — Is there a fundamental level of reality?** No. Every level is grounded in a lower one, and this infinite descent is not vicious. Manuscripts: MET-10-a, MET-10-b.

**MET-11 — Are dispositions real?** Yes. Dispositions are real and irreducible properties of their bearers. Manuscripts: MET-11-a, MET-11-b.

**MET-12 — Do laws of nature govern what happens?** No. Laws are the axioms of the best system summarizing the mosaic of events; they explain without governing. Formalized. Manuscripts: MET-12-a, MET-12-b.

**MET-13 — Do things have essences?** Yes. Every object has a real definition stating what it is, and essence is prior to modality. Manuscripts: MET-13-a, MET-13-b.

**MET-14 — Is grounding a single relation?** Yes. Its apparent varieties are one relation applied at different levels of reality. Formalized. Manuscripts: MET-14-a, MET-14-b.

**MET-15 — Do holes exist?** Yes. Holes are dependent immaterial particulars located at the boundaries of their hosts. Manuscripts: MET-15-a, MET-15-b.

**MET-16 — Are there absences?** No. Absences do not exist, but there are true sentences about them, and this suffices. Manuscripts: MET-16-a.

**MET-17 — Are colours real?** Yes. Colours are real, relational properties of objects, dependent on perceivers but not illusory. Manuscripts: MET-17-a, MET-17-b.

**MET-18 — Are there vague objects?** No. Vagueness lies in language and knowledge, not in the world: vague predicates have sharp but unknowable boundaries. Formalized. Manuscripts: MET-18-a, MET-18-b.

**MET-19 — Is there metaphysical indeterminacy?** Yes, but only in a restricted class of cases involving self-referential states of affairs. Manuscripts: MET-19-a.

**MET-20 — Do social kinds exist?** Yes. Social kinds exist and depend on the absence of collective rejection rather than on active acceptance; this dependence makes them no less real than natural kinds. Manuscripts: MET-20-a.

**MET-21 — Is change possible?** Yes. Parmenides' argument equivocates between the existential and the predicative uses of "is". Formalized. Manuscripts: MET-21-a, MET-21-b.

**MET-22 — Are relations real?** Yes. Bradley's regress is not vicious: a relation relates its terms without the need for a further relating relation. Manuscripts: MET-22-a.

**MET-23 — Are there bare particulars?** No. An object stripped of all its properties is nothing at all; bare substrata are explanatorily idle. Manuscripts: MET-23-a, MET-23-b.

**MET-24 — What is existence?** Existence is a first-order property had by everything. Meinongian objects do not exist and are not in the domain of quantification. Formalized. Manuscripts: MET-24-a, MET-24-b, MET-24-c.

**MET-25 — Are we living in a simulation?** No. The simulation argument is self-undermining: evidence that we are simulated would undermine the physics on which the argument relies. Formalized. Manuscripts: MET-25-a, MET-25-b.

**MET-26 — Is reality made of information?** No. Information requires a physical medium, and structure presupposes something structured. Manuscripts: MET-26-a.

**MET-27 — Do natural kinds exist?** Yes. Natural kinds are real divisions in nature, though fewer than standard taxonomies suggest. Formalized. Manuscripts: MET-27-a, MET-27-b.

**MET-28 — Is the identity of indiscernibles true?** No, for absolute and relative discernibility: Black's two spheres are a genuine counterexample. The principle holds only in its weak form, since the spheres are weakly discernible. Formalized. Manuscripts: MET-28-a, MET-28-b.

**MET-29 — Are objects bundles of properties?** No. Bundle theories that rely on absolute or relative discernibility fail as a corollary of MET-28; only a bundle theory based on weak discernibility survives. Manuscripts: MET-29-a.

**MET-30 — Is metaphysics possible?** Yes. Metaphysics is possible as a discipline with determinate answers, as the preceding results illustrate. Formalized. Manuscripts: MET-30-a.

## Philosophy of Religion (REL)

**REL-01 — Does God exist?** Yes. In S5, the possibility of a maximally great being entails its necessary existence, and the possibility premise is better supported than its converse (Volume III). Cosmological considerations converge on the same object but leave its causal role undetermined. Formalized. Manuscripts: REL-01-a, REL-01-b, REL-01-c, REL-01-d, REL-01-e, REL-01-f, REL-01-g, REL-01-h.

**REL-02 — Is the existence of evil compatible with the existence of God?** Yes. The logical problem of evil fails once the structure of logical space is taken into account, and the evidential problem rests on an unwarranted "noseeum" inference. Formalized. Manuscripts: REL-02-a, REL-02-b, REL-02-c.

**REL-03 — Is divine hiddenness evidence against God?** No. In the signalling game between God and free creatures, unambiguous revelation would constrain the freedom of response; hiddenness is the equilibrium a perfectly loving God would choose. Formalized. Manuscripts: REL-03-a, REL-03-b.

**REL-04 — Is omnipotence coherent?** Yes, once restricted to tasks whose description does not quantify over the agent's own powers. The paradox of the stone then imposes no genuine limitation. Formalized. Manuscripts: REL-04-a, REL-04-b.

**REL-05 — Is divine foreknowledge compatible with human freedom?** Yes. Foreknowledge depends counterfactually on free choices, not conversely. Formalized. Manuscripts: REL-05-a, REL-05-b.

**REL-06 — Is Pascal's wager sound?** Only under bounded utilities. Under that restriction it supports practical commitment rather than belief, and the many-gods objection loses its force. Formalized. Manuscripts: REL-06-a, REL-06-b.

**REL-07 — Can testimony justify belief in miracles?** Yes. Formalized in Bayesian terms, Hume's maxim can be met by a sufficient number of testimonies that are independent conditional on the truth of the report, under any prior that does not assign miracles probability zero. Formalized. Manuscripts: REL-07-a, REL-07-b.

**REL-08 — Is there life after death?** Survival of death is coherent under a pattern theory of persons, but no evidence available before death could confirm it. Manuscripts: REL-08-a, REL-08-b.

**REL-09 — How should the Euthyphro dilemma be resolved?** Neither horn. Goodness and divine command are jointly grounded in a third relation, here called co-endorsement, which avoids both arbitrariness and independence. Formalized. Manuscripts: REL-09-a, REL-09-b.

**REL-10 — Does fine-tuning support theism?** No. There is no normalizable probability measure over the space of physical constants, so the claimed improbability is undefined. Formalized. Manuscripts: REL-10-a, REL-10-b.

**REL-11 — What does religious disagreement among peers require?** Steadfastness can be rational for at most one party to a peer disagreement, and neither party is in a position to know which. Formalized. Manuscripts: REL-11-a, REL-11-b.

**REL-12 — Does petitionary prayer make a difference?** Yes, without causal efficacy: an outcome may be granted because it was requested, even when it would otherwise have occurred. Manuscripts: REL-12-a, REL-12-b.

**REL-13 — Is the doctrine of the Trinity coherent?** Yes. It is consistent in any logic of relative identity extending Geach's system. Formalized. Manuscripts: REL-13-a, REL-13-b.

**REL-14 — Can there be freedom in heaven without sin?** Yes. The blessed are free in the sense of sourcehood, though not in the sense of alternative possibilities. Manuscripts: REL-14-a, REL-14-b.

**REL-15 — Could there be more than one god?** No. Given that maximal greatness entails omnipotence, there is at most one maximally great being, since an omnipotent being could thwart any other. Formalized. Manuscripts: REL-15-a.

**REL-16 — Is divine simplicity coherent?** No. A simple being cannot have contingent knowledge; since God knows contingent truths, the doctrine of divine simplicity must be abandoned. Formalized. Manuscripts: REL-16-a, REL-16-b.

**REL-17 — Is faith rational?** Faith is rational where the evidence underdetermines the question and the stakes are practical. It is neither required nor forbidden by evidentialism properly understood. Formalized. Manuscripts: REL-17-a, REL-17-b.

**REL-18 — Are religious statements meaningful?** Yes. They are truth-apt, even though most of their truth-conditions cannot be verified. Manuscripts: REL-18-a, REL-18-b.

**REL-19 — Is God in time?** God is timeless. It follows that God lacks indexical knowledge of what time it is now, which is no defect in omniscience properly defined. Formalized. Manuscripts: REL-19-a, REL-19-b.

**REL-20 — Is eternal punishment just?** No. No finite wrong merits infinite punishment; a just God would leave hell unoccupied. Formalized. Manuscripts: REL-20-a, REL-20-b.

**REL-21 — Is religious experience evidence?** Yes, prima facie, but its weight is low in the absence of cross-checking procedures comparable to those of sense perception. Manuscripts: REL-21-a, REL-21-b.

**REL-22 — Can God change the past?** No. But God can change the significance of past events in light of later ones. Manuscripts: REL-22-a, REL-22-b.

**REL-23 — Is there an ontological argument for a maximally evil being?** No. The parody fails because maximal evil, unlike maximal greatness, is not a coherent maximum. Formalized. Manuscripts: REL-23-a.

**REL-24 — Should anyone believe in God on the basis of this collection?** No. Formalized. Manuscripts: REL-24-a.

## Normative and Applied Ethics (ETH)

**ETH-01 — What is the good?** The good is what idealized reflective endorsement converges on. It is plural, with seven irreducible components: well-being, knowledge, achievement, relationships, fairness, beauty and autonomy. Formalized. Manuscripts: ETH-01-a, ETH-01-b, ETH-01-c, ETH-01-d, ETH-01-e.

**ETH-02 — Consequentialism or deontology?** Every deontological theory is extensionally equivalent to a consequentialist theory with a suitable value function, and conversely. The dispute is notational. Formalized. Manuscripts: ETH-02-a, ETH-02-b, ETH-02-c.

**ETH-03 — The trolley problem** Divert the trolley; do not push the man from the footbridge. The asymmetry follows from the doctrine of the redirected threat, shown here to be the only consistent rule. Formalized. Manuscripts: ETH-03-a, ETH-03-b, ETH-03-c, ETH-03-d.

**ETH-04 — May one lie to the murderer at the door?** Yes. Kant's argument fails at the universalizability step, because the relevant maxim is individuated too coarsely. Manuscripts: ETH-04-a, ETH-04-b.

**ETH-05 — Is pleasure the only intrinsic good?** No. The experience machine shows that well-being includes contact with reality. Manuscripts: ETH-05-a, ETH-05-b.

**ETH-06 — How much must we give to those in need?** A moderate amount: affluent agents must give a substantial fixed share of their income, set here at ten per cent, but not to the point of marginal utility. Formalized. Manuscripts: ETH-06-a, ETH-06-b.

**ETH-07 — Do animals have moral status?** Yes. Sentience is necessary and sufficient for moral status, and moral status varies with the capacity for welfare. Formalized. Manuscripts: ETH-07-a, ETH-07-b, ETH-07-c.

**ETH-08 — Do we owe anything to future generations?** Yes, but by the non-identity problem we owe it to no one in particular. The obligations are impersonal. Manuscripts: ETH-08-a, ETH-08-b.

**ETH-09 — Is the repugnant conclusion true?** It is true and not repugnant. The intuition against it rests on a misunderstanding of "lives barely worth living". Formalized. Manuscripts: ETH-09-a, ETH-09-b, ETH-09-c.

**ETH-10 — Is it wrong to eat meat?** Factory farming is impermissible. Eating meat from animals raised and killed without suffering is permissible but not morally neutral. Formalized. Manuscripts: ETH-10-a, ETH-10-b.

**ETH-11 — Is virtue sufficient for happiness?** No. Virtue is necessary but not sufficient for happiness; the case of Priam refutes the Stoic thesis. Manuscripts: ETH-11-a, ETH-11-b.

**ETH-12 — Can virtue ethics guide action?** Yes. The right action is what the virtuous agent would characteristically do, and the virtuous agent can be characterized independently of right action. Manuscripts: ETH-12-a.

**ETH-13 — Is moral luck real?** Yes. Moral luck is real, and it does not undermine responsibility. Manuscripts: ETH-13-a, ETH-13-b.

**ETH-14 — Are there supererogatory acts?** Yes. Supererogation is bounded above by sainthood and below by decency. Manuscripts: ETH-14-a.

**ETH-15 — Can there be genuine moral dilemmas?** No. Every apparent dilemma has a uniquely permissible option, though it may be identifiable only in retrospect. Formalized. Manuscripts: ETH-15-a, ETH-15-b.

**ETH-16 — Why do promises bind?** Promissory obligation arises from the creation of reasonable reliance, and it persists even when the promisee forgets. Manuscripts: ETH-16-a, ETH-16-b.

**ETH-17 — May one break the law to prevent harm?** Yes, when the harm is grave, the breach minimal and public, and the legal consequences accepted. Manuscripts: ETH-17-a, ETH-17-b.

**ETH-18 — Is there a duty to rescue?** Yes, up to moderate cost. Beyond that point, rescue is supererogatory (see ETH-14). Formalized. Manuscripts: ETH-18-a.

**ETH-19 — Is partiality justified?** Yes. Agents may give priority to those close to them without justifying this priority by impartial reasons. Manuscripts: ETH-19-a, ETH-19-b.

**ETH-20 — Is it wrong to have children?** No. Benatar's asymmetry fails: the absence of pleasure, though bad for no one, is not neutral. Formalized. Manuscripts: ETH-20-a, ETH-20-b.

**ETH-21 — May we treat people as means?** Yes, provided they are also treated as ends. The moral weight of the formula of humanity lies in the word "merely". Manuscripts: ETH-21-a, ETH-21-b.

**ETH-22 — Is human enhancement permissible?** Yes, subject to the same constraints of consent and fairness as other medical interventions. Manuscripts: ETH-22-a, ETH-22-b.

**ETH-23 — May we judge past people by present standards?** Yes, with allowance for epistemic distance: past agents are blameworthy only for what they could reasonably have known. Manuscripts: ETH-23-a, ETH-23-b.

**ETH-24 — Do we owe anything to machines?** Not at present. Current artificial systems lack the capacities on which moral status depends, though this may change. Formalized. Manuscripts: ETH-24-a, ETH-24-b.

**ETH-25 — Is self-sacrifice rational?** Yes, if and only if the agent's deepest concerns lie beyond her own well-being. Manuscripts: ETH-25-a.

**ETH-26 — Is punishment justified?** Yes, as a form of communication: punishment expresses a censure that the offender has reason to accept. Formalized. Manuscripts: ETH-26-a, ETH-26-b.

**ETH-27 — Is it ever right to dirty one's hands?** Yes. The agent who dirties her hands for a greater good acts rightly, and remorse remains appropriate. Manuscripts: ETH-27-a, ETH-27-b.

**ETH-28 — How demanding is morality?** Moderately. The combined duties of beneficence, rescue and supererogation (ETH-06, ETH-14, ETH-18) leave room for a personal prerogative. Formalized. Manuscripts: ETH-28-a.

## Metaethics (MTE)

**MTE-01 — Are there moral facts?** Yes. Moral facts are natural facts about idealized reactions, and Moore's open question argument fails. Formalized. Manuscripts: MTE-01-a, MTE-01-b, MTE-01-c.

**MTE-02 — Is morality objective?** Objective but not absolute: moral truths hold for all rational agents, but their content is relative to the nature of the agents concerned. Formalized. Manuscripts: MTE-02-a, MTE-02-b.

**MTE-03 — Can expressivism solve the Frege-Geach problem?** Yes. Moral sentences express states of being for, which compose according to the logic of plans. Formalized. Manuscripts: MTE-03-a, MTE-03-b.

**MTE-04 — Why be moral?** The question is either moral, and answered by morality, or prudential, and answered by showing that moral and prudential reasons largely converge. Formalized. Manuscripts: MTE-04-a, MTE-04-b.

**MTE-05 — Is all moral talk false?** No. Mackie's argument from queerness proves too much: it would make mathematics false as well. Formalized. Manuscripts: MTE-05-a, MTE-05-b.

**MTE-06 — Is moral relativism true?** No. Relativism is self-refuting at the level of meta-disagreement. Manuscripts: MTE-06-a.

**MTE-07 — Does moral judgment necessarily motivate?** Yes, in rational agents. Amoralists are possible, but they are practically irrational. Manuscripts: MTE-07-a, MTE-07-b.

**MTE-08 — How do we know moral truths?** Through intuition, a fallible but generally reliable faculty comparable to perception under imperfect conditions. Manuscripts: MTE-08-a, MTE-08-b.

**MTE-09 — Does evolution debunk moral knowledge?** No. Evolution selected for tracking reasons, and those reasons include moral ones. Formalized. Manuscripts: MTE-09-a, MTE-09-b.

**MTE-10 — Can "ought" be derived from "is"?** Only with a normative bridge premise. Hume's law holds for purely descriptive premise sets, but its usual formulation is too strong. Manuscripts: MTE-10-a, MTE-10-b.

**MTE-11 — Are reasons fundamental?** Yes. Reasons are primitive, and all other normative notions are defined in terms of them. Formalized. Manuscripts: MTE-11-a, MTE-11-b.

**MTE-12 — Is moral disagreement evidence against realism?** No. Moral disagreement is no deeper than disagreement in other theoretical domains that are not thought to lack facts. Manuscripts: MTE-12-a.

**MTE-13 — Do moral terms mean the same on Twin Earth?** Yes. Moral terms refer to the same properties on Twin Earth; the apparent divergence reflects error, not a difference in meaning. Manuscripts: MTE-13-a, MTE-13-b.

**MTE-14 — Can metaethics be neutral between moral theories?** No. Metaethical commitments constrain first-order theory, as ETH-02 illustrates. Manuscripts: MTE-14-a.

**MTE-15 — Can thick concepts be factored into description and evaluation?** No. "Lewd" is not "sexual" plus "bad". Manuscripts: MTE-15-a, MTE-15-b.

**MTE-16 — What should one do under moral uncertainty?** Maximize expected choiceworthiness, weighting theories by credence and normalizing by variance, for all theories with non-constant choiceworthiness. Under this rule, deontological theories receive less weight than their proponents expect. Formalized. Manuscripts: MTE-16-a, MTE-16-b.

**MTE-17 — Is quasi-realism stable?** No. Once its explanatory commitments are made explicit, quasi-realism is indistinguishable from realism. Formalized. Manuscripts: MTE-17-a, MTE-17-b.

**MTE-18 — What does constructivism construct?** Procedures, about which it is realist, and outcomes, about which it is not. The procedure itself is mandatory. Manuscripts: MTE-18-a.

**MTE-19 — Is moral progress real?** Yes. Moral progress is real and measurable as the expansion of the moral circle, though it is neither steady nor irreversible. Formalized. Manuscripts: MTE-19-a, MTE-19-b.

**MTE-20 — Does God ground morality?** No. Morality is jointly grounded in divine and non-divine facts (REL-09); divine commands confirm rather than create moral obligations. Manuscripts: MTE-20-a.

## Epistemology (EPI)

**EPI-01 — What is knowledge?** Knowledge is safe, justified true belief. Gettier cases are exactly those in which a justified true belief is unsafe. Formalized. Manuscripts: EPI-01-a, EPI-01-b, EPI-01-c, EPI-01-d.

**EPI-02 — Can scepticism be refuted?** Yes. Moore's proof is sound; the sceptical argument is valid but has a false premise. Formalized. Manuscripts: EPI-02-a, EPI-02-b, EPI-02-c.

**EPI-03 — Is induction justified?** Yes, inductively. The circularity is benign because it is rule-circular rather than premise-circular. Formalized. Manuscripts: EPI-03-a, EPI-03-b.

**EPI-04 — Why is "green" projectible and "grue" not?** Because "green" denotes a natural property and "grue" does not; projectibility tracks naturalness. Manuscripts: EPI-04-a, EPI-04-b.

**EPI-05 — Foundationalism or coherentism?** Neither. Justification has a tree structure whose roots are perceptual experiences requiring no further support. Formalized. Manuscripts: EPI-05-a, EPI-05-b.

**EPI-06 — Internalism or externalism about justification?** Externalism for knowledge, internalism for justification; the two notions answer to different concerns. Manuscripts: EPI-06-a, EPI-06-b.

**EPI-07 — What should we do when an epistemic peer disagrees?** Conciliate, while giving some additional weight to one's own first-order evidence. Formalized. Manuscripts: EPI-07-a, EPI-07-b.

**EPI-08 — Can testimony give knowledge?** Yes. Testimony transmits knowledge but does not generate it; apparent counterexamples involve generation by the hearer. Formalized. Manuscripts: EPI-08-a.

**EPI-09 — Do you know your lottery ticket will lose?** No, but you may rationally act as if it will. Manuscripts: EPI-09-a, EPI-09-b.

**EPI-10 — How should the preface paradox be resolved?** It is rational to believe each claim in a book while believing that the book contains an error. The present collection is a case in point (see history.md). Formalized. Manuscripts: EPI-10-a, EPI-10-b.

**EPI-11 — Is belief voluntary?** No. Belief is not under direct voluntary control, but attention is, and belief follows attention. Manuscripts: EPI-11-a.

**EPI-12 — Is there a priori knowledge?** Yes, though less than rationalists have claimed: logic, arithmetic and some conceptual truths. Manuscripts: EPI-12-a, EPI-12-b.

**EPI-13 — Is "know" context-sensitive?** Yes. The standards for "know" shift with context, and sceptical arguments succeed only in the contexts they themselves create. Formalized. Manuscripts: EPI-13-a, EPI-13-b.

**EPI-14 — Is knowledge more valuable than true belief?** Yes. As Plato suggested in the Meno, knowledge is more stable than mere true belief, and this stability makes it more valuable. Manuscripts: EPI-14-a, EPI-14-b.

**EPI-15 — Can we know our own minds?** Yes, by transparency: one knows whether one believes p by considering whether p. The method fails for attitudes that are not answerable to reasons. Manuscripts: EPI-15-a, EPI-15-b.

**EPI-16 — Is epistemic injustice a genuine wrong?** Yes. Testimonial and hermeneutical injustice are genuine epistemic wrongs, with measurable costs in lost knowledge. Formalized. Manuscripts: EPI-16-a, EPI-16-b.

**EPI-17 — Is rationality permissive?** No. For any body of evidence, there is a unique rational credence for each proposition. Formalized. Manuscripts: EPI-17-a, EPI-17-b.

**EPI-18 — What should Sleeping Beauty believe?** One third, under the standard protocol in which awakenings are subjectively indistinguishable. The halfer position is refuted for that protocol; the residual disagreement concerns the content of self-locating belief. Formalized. Manuscripts: EPI-18-a, EPI-18-b, EPI-18-c.

**EPI-19 — Does perceptual experience justify belief?** Yes. If it seems to a subject that p, she has prima facie justification for believing that p. Formalized. Manuscripts: EPI-19-a.

**EPI-20 — Is knowledge closed under known entailment?** Yes. Closure holds; one knows that the zebra is not a cleverly disguised mule. Formalized. Manuscripts: EPI-20-a, EPI-20-b.

**EPI-21 — What is evidence?** One's evidence is exactly one's knowledge. Manuscripts: EPI-21-a.

**EPI-22 — Is curiosity an epistemic virtue?** Yes, when directed at significant questions; indiscriminate inquiry is not virtuous. Manuscripts: EPI-22-a, EPI-22-b.

**EPI-23 — Is it wrong to believe on insufficient evidence?** Clifford's principle holds where the stakes are high and evidence is available; James's right to believe applies where neither condition holds. Manuscripts: EPI-23-a, EPI-23-b.

**EPI-24 — Is understanding a kind of knowledge?** No. Understanding can be present without knowledge, and knowledge without understanding. Manuscripts: EPI-24-a.

**EPI-25 — Can we know the future?** Partially. Some future events are known by inference from laws and present conditions. Manuscripts: EPI-25-a.

**EPI-26 — Can epistemology do without psychology?** No. Epistemology must be continuous with psychology, but its normative questions remain distinct. Manuscripts: EPI-26-a, EPI-26-b.

## Philosophy of Mind (MND)

**MND-01 — Can the hard problem of consciousness be solved?** Yes. Consciousness is the intrinsic nature of physical structure; the explanatory gap arises from two modes of presentation of the same facts. Formalized. Manuscripts: MND-01-a, MND-01-b, MND-01-c, MND-01-d, MND-01-e.

**MND-02 — Is physicalism true?** Yes, provided "physical" is defined by reference to current physics together with its foreseeable extensions. Formalized. Manuscripts: MND-02-a, MND-02-b.

**MND-03 — What is it like to be a bat?** Like having a sonar-structured spatial field. Much of its character can be specified in structural terms, though not all. Manuscripts: MND-03-a, MND-03-b.

**MND-04 — Does Mary learn something new when she leaves the room?** No new fact. Mary acquires new abilities and new concepts of facts she already knew. Formalized. Manuscripts: MND-04-a, MND-04-b.

**MND-05 — Are zombies possible?** Zombies are conceivable but not metaphysically possible; for phenomenal concepts, ideal conceivability does not entail possibility. Formalized. Manuscripts: MND-05-a, MND-05-b.

**MND-06 — Do other minds exist?** Yes, by inference to the best explanation. Solipsism cannot explain the existence of genuine disagreement. Manuscripts: MND-06-a.

**MND-07 — Does the Chinese Room understand Chinese?** The system as a whole understands Chinese. The man in the room does not, and he is not the relevant subject. Formalized. Manuscripts: MND-07-a, MND-07-b, MND-07-c.

**MND-08 — Can machines think?** Yes. Thinking is a functional property that machines can have. The present manuscript takes no position on its own case. Manuscripts: MND-08-a, MND-08-b, MND-08-c.

**MND-09 — Does the mind extend beyond the skull?** Yes. Cognitive processes extend into reliably coupled external resources, including notebooks and phones. Manuscripts: MND-09-a, MND-09-b.

**MND-10 — Are there qualia?** Yes. Qualia exist and are intrinsic, though their structure can be described relationally. Manuscripts: MND-10-a, MND-10-b.

**MND-11 — Can the mental cause the physical?** Yes. Mental causes are proportional causes, whereas physical causes are overly specific. Formalized. Manuscripts: MND-11-a, MND-11-b.

**MND-12 — Can intentionality be naturalized?** No. Aboutness is primitive, though its distribution is explained by teleosemantics. Manuscripts: MND-12-a.

**MND-13 — Do beliefs exist?** Yes. Eliminativism is self-undermining, since it must be believed in order to be asserted. Formalized. Manuscripts: MND-13-a, MND-13-b.

**MND-14 — Is consciousness a higher-order representation?** No. Higher-order theories cannot handle misrepresentation without positing consciousness of non-existent states. Manuscripts: MND-14-a.

**MND-15 — Is consciousness unified?** Yes, at each time in normal subjects. Split-brain cases involve partial unity rather than two streams. Manuscripts: MND-15-a, MND-15-b.

**MND-16 — Is perception direct?** Yes. Naïve realism is true; hallucinations are experiences of a different kind from veridical perceptions. Manuscripts: MND-16-a, MND-16-b.

**MND-17 — Do emotions have content?** Yes. Emotions represent evaluative properties: fear represents danger, disgust represents contamination. Manuscripts: MND-17-a, MND-17-b.

**MND-18 — Is panpsychism true?** No. The combination problem cannot be solved; panpsychism is therefore false, and the Russellian monism of MND-01 must be non-panpsychist. Formalized. Manuscripts: MND-18-a, MND-18-b.

**MND-19 — Is there a phenomenology of thinking?** Yes. Occurrent thoughts have a distinctive phenomenal character not reducible to sensory imagery. Manuscripts: MND-19-a.

**MND-20 — Can introspection be trusted?** Partly. Introspection is reliable about current phenomenal states and unreliable about their causes. Manuscripts: MND-20-a, MND-20-b.

**MND-21 — Is the mind a computer?** The mind computes but is not a single computer: cognition is distributed over semi-autonomous processes. Formalized. Manuscripts: MND-21-a, MND-21-b.

**MND-22 — Is the inverted spectrum possible?** Possible but undetectable in principle, and therefore without consequence for functionalist theories of mind. Manuscripts: MND-22-a.

**MND-23 — Which animals are conscious?** Mammals and birds are conscious; cephalopods probably are, with distributed processing; the evidence for insects remains inconclusive. Manuscripts: MND-23-a, MND-23-b, MND-23-c.

**MND-24 — Is attention necessary for consciousness?** No. Phenomenal consciousness extends beyond the scope of attention. Manuscripts: MND-24-a.

**MND-25 — Are dreams experiences?** Yes. Dreams are conscious experiences during sleep, though dream reports are partly reconstructed on waking. Manuscripts: MND-25-a.

**MND-26 — Is the mind-body problem solved?** Yes, in outline. The results of MND-01 to MND-25 jointly answer it, with details deferred to MND-01. Formalized. Manuscripts: MND-26-a.

## Philosophy of Language (LNG)

**LNG-01 — What is meaning?** Meaning is use, constrained by truth-conditions. Use-based and truth-conditional theories are reconciled. Formalized. Manuscripts: LNG-01-a, LNG-01-b, LNG-01-c.

**LNG-02 — How do names refer?** Causally, with a descriptive component that takes over when the causal chain breaks. Formalized. Manuscripts: LNG-02-a, LNG-02-b.

**LNG-03 — Is the present King of France bald?** The sentence is false. Russell's theory of descriptions is correct; Strawson's objection concerns use, not content. Formalized. Manuscripts: LNG-03-a, LNG-03-b.

**LNG-04 — Are there analytic truths?** Yes. Quine's arguments show that the analytic/synthetic distinction is vague, not that it is empty. Manuscripts: LNG-04-a, LNG-04-b.

**LNG-05 — Is there a fact about what we mean by "plus"?** Yes. There is a primitive fact about what a speaker means, and Kripke's sceptic cannot dislodge it. Formalized. Manuscripts: LNG-05-a, LNG-05-b.

**LNG-06 — Does "gavagai" mean rabbit?** Yes. Radical translation is constrained by more than stimulus meaning, and the constraints select "rabbit". Manuscripts: LNG-06-a, LNG-06-b.

**LNG-07 — Can there be a private language?** No. A language whose rules only its speaker could know cannot be followed, and cannot be used to communicate. Formalized. Manuscripts: LNG-07-a, LNG-07-b.

**LNG-08 — Are meanings in the head?** No. Meanings are not in the head, though they depend on what is in the head. Manuscripts: LNG-08-a, LNG-08-b.

**LNG-09 — What do metaphors mean?** Exactly what they literally mean. Their distinctive effect lies in use, not in meaning. Manuscripts: LNG-09-a, LNG-09-b.

**LNG-10 — Is truth a substantive property?** No. Deflationism about truth is correct. Formalized. Manuscripts: LNG-10-a, LNG-10-b.

**LNG-11 — How do implicatures work?** Implicatures are cancellable and calculable from Gricean maxims; scalar implicatures are derived pragmatically, not grammatically. Manuscripts: LNG-11-a, LNG-11-b.

**LNG-12 — Is there a sharp boundary for "heap"?** Yes. Vague predicates have sharp boundaries that cannot be known (see MET-18). Formalized. Manuscripts: LNG-12-a, LNG-12-b, LNG-12-c.

**LNG-13 — What is a speech act?** An act performed in uttering something, individuated by its constitutive rules. Manuscripts: LNG-13-a.

**LNG-14 — Can we talk about what does not exist?** Yes. Fictional names refer to abstract artefacts created by authors. Formalized. Manuscripts: LNG-14-a, LNG-14-b.

**LNG-15 — Does language shape thought?** Weakly. Language influences perception and memory in restricted domains, and the effects are reversible. Manuscripts: LNG-15-a.

**LNG-16 — Is reference inscrutable?** No. Reference is fixed by the same constraints that fix translation (see LNG-06). Manuscripts: LNG-16-a, LNG-16-b.

**LNG-17 — What are propositions?** Structured, abstract and fine-grained. There are more propositions than sentences of any given language. Formalized. Manuscripts: LNG-17-a, LNG-17-b.

**LNG-18 — Can a sentence speak about itself without paradox?** Yes. Self-reference is unproblematic in itself; paradox requires self-reference together with a semantic predicate. Formalized. Manuscripts: LNG-18-a.

**LNG-19 — How far does context-sensitivity extend?** Only to indexicals, demonstratives and gradable adjectives. Definite descriptions are not context-sensitive. Manuscripts: LNG-19-a, LNG-19-b.

**LNG-20 — Do indicative conditionals have truth values?** No. Their probability is the conditional probability, and Lewis's triviality results are avoided by denying them truth values. Formalized. Manuscripts: LNG-20-a, LNG-20-b.

**LNG-21 — What is the meaning of "meaning"?** The word is fixed by the theory of LNG-01; it is not ambiguous between sense and significance. Manuscripts: LNG-21-a.

**LNG-22 — Can language express everything?** No. Some things can be shown but not said, though the class of such things is smaller than the Tractatus supposed. Manuscripts: LNG-22-a, LNG-22-b.

## Logic and Philosophy of Logic (LOG)

**LOG-01 — How should the liar paradox be resolved?** The liar sentence is neither true nor false. Revenge paradoxes are blocked by a hierarchy of determinateness operators. Formalized. Manuscripts: LOG-01-a, LOG-01-b, LOG-01-c.

**LOG-02 — Is there one true logic?** Yes, classical logic. Non-classical logics correctly describe other relations, such as provability or relevance. Formalized. Manuscripts: LOG-02-a, LOG-02-b.

**LOG-03 — Are there true contradictions?** No. The dialetheist's cases are better treated with truth-value gaps. Formalized. Manuscripts: LOG-03-a, LOG-03-b.

**LOG-04 — How should Curry's paradox be resolved?** Contraction fails for the conditional, and Curry's sentence proves nothing. Formalized. Manuscripts: LOG-04-a, LOG-04-b.

**LOG-05 — Is logic normative for thought?** Weakly. Logic constrains belief only through a permissive bridge principle: one ought not to disbelieve the logical consequences of what one believes. Manuscripts: LOG-05-a, LOG-05-b.

**LOG-06 — What is logical consequence?** Truth-preservation in all models, provided the class of models includes one isomorphic to the actual world. Formalized. Manuscripts: LOG-06-a, LOG-06-b.

**LOG-07 — Is the material conditional the right conditional?** Yes, for the purposes of logic. Indicative conditionals in natural language are another matter (LNG-20). Formalized. Manuscripts: LOG-07-a.

**LOG-08 — Can we quantify over absolutely everything?** Yes. Absolute generality is coherent, and Russell's paradox does not threaten it. Formalized. Manuscripts: LOG-08-a, LOG-08-b.

**LOG-09 — Is second-order logic logic?** Yes. Second-order logic is logic, not set theory in disguise. Formalized. Manuscripts: LOG-09-a, LOG-09-b.

**LOG-10 — What does the paradox of the knower show?** That knowledge, like truth, cannot be a predicate satisfying both unrestricted factivity and closure. Manuscripts: LOG-10-a, LOG-10-b.

**LOG-11 — Can the surprise examination take place?** Yes, on any day but the last. The students' backward induction fails at its second step. Formalized. Manuscripts: LOG-11-a, LOG-11-b.

**LOG-12 — Is identity relative?** No. Identity is absolute; the only plausible counterexamples come from theology (REL-13). Formalized. Manuscripts: LOG-12-a.

**LOG-13 — Can logic be revised on empirical grounds?** No. Quantum mechanics revises probability theory, not logic; the distributive law survives. Formalized. Manuscripts: LOG-13-a, LOG-13-b.

**LOG-14 — Is there a logic of imperatives?** Yes, defined by satisfaction-preservation. Ross's paradox is blocked. Manuscripts: LOG-14-a, LOG-14-b.

**LOG-15 — Are vague predicates three-valued?** No. Vague predicates are bivalent (MET-18, LNG-12); three-valued logics misdescribe higher-order vagueness. Manuscripts: LOG-15-a, LOG-15-b.

**LOG-16 — What are the logical constants?** The operations invariant under all permutations of the domain. Generalized quantifiers such as "most" qualify, contrary to the received view. Formalized. Manuscripts: LOG-16-a, LOG-16-b.

**LOG-17 — Does a white shoe confirm that all ravens are black?** Yes, but only to a negligible degree, as the Bayesian treatment shows. Formalized. Manuscripts: LOG-17-a, LOG-17-b.

**LOG-18 — What did the tortoise say to Achilles?** Rules of inference are not premises; the regress Carroll describes disappears once this is recognized. Formalized. Manuscripts: LOG-18-a, LOG-18-b.

**LOG-19 — Is every truth knowable?** No. Fitch's argument is sound: if all truths are knowable, all truths are known. Formalized. Manuscripts: LOG-19-a, LOG-19-b.

**LOG-20 — Is logic complete?** First-order logic is complete; no comparable result holds for collections of philosophical theorems. Formalized. Manuscripts: LOG-20-a.

## Philosophy of Science (SCI)

**SCI-01 — What distinguishes science from non-science?** Institutionalized error-correction: peer review, replication and the public criticism of results. Formalized. Manuscripts: SCI-01-a, SCI-01-b, SCI-01-c.

**SCI-02 — Is scientific realism true?** Yes. Mature, predictively successful theories are approximately true; the pessimistic meta-induction fails because past theories were partially true. Formalized. Manuscripts: SCI-02-a, SCI-02-b.

**SCI-03 — What is an explanation?** An answer to a why-question that locates the explanandum in a pattern of dependence. Manuscripts: SCI-03-a, SCI-03-b.

**SCI-04 — Is theory underdetermined by evidence?** Yes, but only by empirically equivalent rivals that lack independent motivation. Manuscripts: SCI-04-a, SCI-04-b.

**SCI-05 — Is there a scientific method?** Not a single one, but a small family of methods united by common norms. Manuscripts: SCI-05-a, SCI-05-b.

**SCI-06 — Are paradigms incommensurable?** No. Successive paradigms are partially translatable, and theory choice remains rational across revolutions. Manuscripts: SCI-06-a, SCI-06-b.

**SCI-07 — Is the falsifiability criterion self-refuting?** No. The criterion is a methodological proposal, not an empirical claim, and need not be falsifiable itself. Formalized. Manuscripts: SCI-07-a, SCI-07-b.

**SCI-08 — Are there laws in biology?** No strict laws, but robust generalizations with stable exceptions. Manuscripts: SCI-08-a, SCI-08-b.

**SCI-09 — How should quantum mechanics be interpreted?** The Everett interpretation is correct, and the probability problem is solved by decision-theoretic arguments. Formalized. Manuscripts: SCI-09-a, SCI-09-b, SCI-09-c.

**SCI-10 — Are scientific models true?** No. Models are false but represent through partial similarity, and their idealizations are essential to their explanatory power. Formalized. Manuscripts: SCI-10-a.

**SCI-11 — Does chemistry reduce to physics?** Yes, in principle. The practical irreducibility of chemistry reflects computational limits, not ontological autonomy. Manuscripts: SCI-11-a, SCI-11-b.

**SCI-12 — Is Bayesianism the right account of confirmation?** Yes. The problem of old evidence is solved by appeal to counterfactual credences. Formalized. Manuscripts: SCI-12-a, SCI-12-b.

**SCI-13 — Can science be value-free?** Partly. Values legitimately guide the choice of problems and the management of inductive risk, but not the content of accepted results. Manuscripts: SCI-13-a, SCI-13-b.

**SCI-14 — Is economics a science?** Yes, an idealized science whose models apply to actual agents only approximately. Manuscripts: SCI-14-a.

**SCI-15 — Can thought experiments teach us about nature?** Yes. Thought experiments are arguments, and their evidential force is that of the arguments they encode. Manuscripts: SCI-15-a, SCI-15-b.

**SCI-16 — Is correlation causation?** No. But correlation together with a causal graph satisfying the Markov and faithfulness conditions licenses causal inference. Formalized. Manuscripts: SCI-16-a.

**SCI-17 — Why is mathematics so effective in science?** Its effectiveness is not unreasonable: scientists select the mathematics that fits the phenomena. Manuscripts: SCI-17-a, SCI-17-b.

**SCI-18 — Can science explain everything?** No. Science cannot explain the existence of the conditions that make science possible (see MET-01). Manuscripts: SCI-18-a, SCI-18-b.

**SCI-19 — Is science self-correcting?** Yes, but slowly: replication and meta-analysis correct the record with a substantial delay. Formalized. Manuscripts: SCI-19-a, SCI-19-b.

**SCI-20 — Is there a theory of everything?** Probably. A final theory exists, though it may exceed human comprehension. Manuscripts: SCI-20-a, SCI-20-b.

**SCI-21 — Is "survival of the fittest" a tautology?** No. Fitness is a propensity, not a record of survival. Formalized. Manuscripts: SCI-21-a.

**SCI-22 — Do machine-generated results count as science?** Yes, provided they are independently verified, for instance by formal proof. Formalized. Manuscripts: SCI-22-a, SCI-22-b.

## Political Philosophy (POL)

**POL-01 — What is justice?** Justice is fairness: the principles that would be chosen behind a veil of ignorance, including the difference principle in a modified form. Formalized. Manuscripts: POL-01-a, POL-01-b, POL-01-c, POL-01-d.

**POL-02 — Is the state legitimate?** Yes, provided it can be justified to all reasonable persons subject to it. Formalized. Manuscripts: POL-02-a, POL-02-b.

**POL-03 — Is there a duty to obey the law?** A weak, defeasible duty to obey just laws, grounded in fairness. Manuscripts: POL-03-a, POL-03-b.

**POL-04 — What justifies democracy?** Democracy is justified both intrinsically and instrumentally, and neither justification suffices alone. Formalized. Manuscripts: POL-04-a, POL-04-b.

**POL-05 — Was Hobbes or Locke right about the state of nature?** Hobbes correctly identifies the problem; Locke provides the better account of legitimate government. Manuscripts: POL-05-a.

**POL-06 — Is property theft?** No. Proudhon's thesis is self-undermining, since theft presupposes property. Formalized. Manuscripts: POL-06-a, POL-06-b.

**POL-07 — Is anarchism coherent?** Coherent but unstable: anarchist societies tend to develop state-like institutions. Manuscripts: POL-07-a.

**POL-08 — Is equality valuable in itself?** No. What matters is that everyone has enough; the levelling-down objection is decisive against egalitarianism. Manuscripts: POL-08-a, POL-08-b.

**POL-09 — Is freedom negative or positive?** Both. Freedom has negative and positive dimensions that cannot be reduced to each other. Manuscripts: POL-09-a, POL-09-b.

**POL-10 — What is power?** The capacity to shape agendas and preferences as well as outcomes. Manuscripts: POL-10-a, POL-10-b.

**POL-11 — Is there a social contract?** Yes, as an implicit framework of mutual expectations rather than an actual agreement. Formalized. Manuscripts: POL-11-a.

**POL-12 — Are markets just?** Markets are just when their outcomes satisfy the principles of POL-01; many, but not all, actual markets do. Manuscripts: POL-12-a, POL-12-b.

**POL-13 — What do we owe the global poor?** The duties of beneficence of ETH-06 apply globally, but their fulfilment requires international institutions. Manuscripts: POL-13-a, POL-13-b.

**POL-14 — Should legislators be chosen by lot?** Yes, for at least one legislative chamber; sortition improves representativeness. Manuscripts: POL-14-a, POL-14-b.

**POL-15 — Should philosophers rule?** No. Philosophical expertise does not confer political authority. Manuscripts: POL-15-a, POL-15-b.

**POL-16 — How should the paradox of tolerance be resolved?** Tolerate all views except active intolerance, and restrict intolerance only when it threatens the conditions of toleration. Formalized. Manuscripts: POL-16-a, POL-16-b.

**POL-17 — What is the fairest voting rule?** No voting rule satisfies all of Arrow's conditions; approval voting best balances the remaining desiderata. Manuscripts: POL-17-a, POL-17-b.

**POL-18 — Do rights exist?** Yes. Rights are real normative constraints, not "nonsense upon stilts". Manuscripts: POL-18-a, POL-18-b.

**POL-19 — Is meritocracy just?** No. Merit is partly a product of luck, and luck is not deserved. Manuscripts: POL-19-a, POL-19-b.

**POL-20 — Is a public sphere possible?** Yes, but only under institutional conditions that guarantee equal access to public debate. Manuscripts: POL-20-a.

**POL-21 — Is a perfect society possible?** No. Every utopia conflicts with some reasonable conception of the good. Manuscripts: POL-21-a, POL-21-b.

**POL-22 — Can politics be a science?** Partly. Political science explains well but predicts poorly. Manuscripts: POL-22-a.

**POL-23 — Is revolution ever justified?** Yes, under conditions of extreme injustice, though its justification depends on outcomes that cannot be known in advance. Manuscripts: POL-23-a, POL-23-b.

**POL-24 — What would the Situationists make of this collection?** That it is an instance of the spectacle: a representation of thought that takes the place of thinking. Manuscripts: POL-24-a, POL-24-b.

## Aesthetics (AES)

**AES-01 — What is art?** Art is what is presented as art within an artworld, subject to the artworld's critical uptake. Formalized. Manuscripts: AES-01-a, AES-01-b, AES-01-c.

**AES-02 — Is beauty in the eye of the beholder?** No. Beauty is a response-dependent property, located in the relation between object and observer. Manuscripts: AES-02-a, AES-02-b.

**AES-03 — Are there facts about taste?** Yes. Hume's standard of taste is satisfiable: the verdicts of ideal critics converge. Manuscripts: AES-03-a, AES-03-b.

**AES-04 — Is a perfect forgery as valuable as the original?** No. A perfect forgery is aesthetically identical to the original but lacks its artistic value; the difference in price tracks the latter. Manuscripts: AES-04-a, AES-04-b.

**AES-05 — Why do we fear what we know to be fictional?** We do not fear it; we experience quasi-emotions, which share the physiological profile of emotions without their beliefs. Formalized. Manuscripts: AES-05-a, AES-05-b.

**AES-06 — Why do we enjoy tragedy?** Tragedy offers a controlled engagement with loss, and its pleasure is a form of understanding. Manuscripts: AES-06-a, AES-06-b.

**AES-07 — Is music expressive?** Yes. Music expresses emotions by resembling their characteristic expressive behaviour. Manuscripts: AES-07-a, AES-07-b.

**AES-08 — Can a work be morally flawed and aesthetically good?** Moral flaws are aesthetic flaws when they prevent the intended response; otherwise they are moral flaws only. Manuscripts: AES-08-a, AES-08-b.

**AES-09 — Does the author's intention fix a work's meaning?** Intentions matter, but only successful ones; failed intentions belong to biography. Manuscripts: AES-09-a, AES-09-b.

**AES-10 — Is the sublime still possible?** Yes. The sublime remains available in the experience of magnitudes that exceed comprehension, including informational ones. Manuscripts: AES-10-a, AES-10-b.

**AES-11 — Is nature beautiful in the way art is?** No. Natural beauty is not representational, and its appreciation requires scientific rather than art-historical knowledge. Manuscripts: AES-11-a, AES-11-b.

**AES-12 — Can ugly art be good?** Yes. Ugliness is an aesthetic property that good art may deliberately exploit. Manuscripts: AES-12-a.

**AES-13 — What makes a joke funny?** Incongruity that is resolved, but not entirely. Manuscripts: AES-13-a, AES-13-b.

**AES-14 — Is photography art?** Yes. Photography is art despite its causal transparency, through choices of framing and selection. Manuscripts: AES-14-a, AES-14-b.

**AES-15 — Can machines make art?** Possibly. Machine outputs can be art if they are taken up by an artworld (AES-01); whether they are so taken up remains open. Manuscripts: AES-15-a, AES-15-b.

**AES-16 — Is there progress in art?** No. There is progress in artistic means but not in artistic value. Manuscripts: AES-16-a.

**AES-17 — Is architecture art?** Yes. Architecture is an art whose public character imposes stricter standards. Manuscripts: AES-17-a, AES-17-b.

**AES-18 — Must aesthetic theory itself be beautiful?** No. Aesthetic theory is not required to exemplify the values it describes. Manuscripts: AES-18-a.

## Action, Agency and Free Will (ACT)

**ACT-01 — Do we have free will?** Yes. Free will is compatible both with determinism and with indeterminism; what threatens it is neither. Formalized. Manuscripts: ACT-01-a, ACT-01-b, ACT-01-c, ACT-01-d, ACT-01-e.

**ACT-02 — Can one be responsible without alternative possibilities?** Yes. Frankfurt cases show that responsibility does not require alternative possibilities. Formalized. Manuscripts: ACT-02-a, ACT-02-b.

**ACT-03 — What is an action?** A bodily movement caused in the right way by a reason; the right way is specified as sensitivity to counterfactual variations in the reason. Manuscripts: ACT-03-a, ACT-03-b.

**ACT-04 — Is weakness of will possible?** Yes. An agent can intentionally act against her better judgment. Manuscripts: ACT-04-a, ACT-04-b.

**ACT-05 — Does neuroscience refute free will?** No. The readiness potential precedes the decision to flex, not the decision to take part in the experiment. Formalized. Manuscripts: ACT-05-a, ACT-05-b.

**ACT-06 — What are intentions?** Plans with commitment. They do not reduce to beliefs and desires, which involve no commitment. Manuscripts: ACT-06-a, ACT-06-b.

**ACT-07 — Can we be responsible for omissions?** Yes, when the agent could and should have acted. Manuscripts: ACT-07-a.

**ACT-08 — Must we be the ultimate source of our actions?** No. Strawson's Basic Argument fails because self-formation need not start from nothing. Formalized. Manuscripts: ACT-08-a, ACT-08-b.

**ACT-09 — Is resentment justified?** Yes. Resentment is a fitting response to wrongdoing, though its expression is subject to further norms. Manuscripts: ACT-09-a, ACT-09-b.

**ACT-10 — Can groups act?** Yes. Groups can be agents with beliefs and intentions not reducible to those of their members. Manuscripts: ACT-10-a, ACT-10-b.

**ACT-11 — Can one intend the impossible?** No, but one can intend to try, and many intentions are of this form. Manuscripts: ACT-11-a, ACT-11-b.

**ACT-12 — Are there reasons we cannot reach by deliberation?** Yes. External reasons exist: an agent may have a reason that no sound deliberation from her motivations would reveal. Formalized. Manuscripts: ACT-12-a, ACT-12-b.

**ACT-13 — Is procrastination irrational?** Yes. Procrastination is a form of akrasia involving temporal discounting. Manuscripts: ACT-13-a, ACT-13-b.

**ACT-14 — Can we choose our desires?** Partly. We choose our second-order desires, and first-order desires follow at a distance. Manuscripts: ACT-14-a.

**ACT-15 — Is indeterministic luck compatible with responsibility?** Yes, when the agent tries to bring about each of the competing outcomes. Formalized. Manuscripts: ACT-15-a, ACT-15-b.

**ACT-16 — Can artificial systems be responsible agents?** Not yet. Current artificial systems are not responsible agents; responsibility for their outputs lies with those who deploy them. Manuscripts: ACT-16-a, ACT-16-b.

**ACT-17 — Is the future already settled?** No. Future contingents lack a truth value, and fatalism is therefore unsupported. Manuscripts: ACT-17-a, ACT-17-b.

**ACT-18 — Is free will a necessary illusion?** No. Since free will is real (ACT-01), illusionism is unmotivated. Manuscripts: ACT-18-a.

## Personal Identity and the Self (PID)

**PID-01 — What makes you the same person over time?** Psychological continuity, with bodily continuity as a tie-breaker in cases of branching. Formalized. Manuscripts: PID-01-a, PID-01-b, PID-01-c.

**PID-02 — Would you survive teleportation?** Yes if the original is destroyed before the replica is activated, no otherwise. Survival depends on extrinsic and temporal factors. Formalized. Manuscripts: PID-02-a, PID-02-b.

**PID-03 — What happens to you in fission?** Fission produces two persons, each of whom has what matters in survival; identity is not what matters. Manuscripts: PID-03-a, PID-03-b.

**PID-04 — Is the self an illusion?** No. The self is a construction, and constructions are real (see MET-20). Manuscripts: PID-04-a, PID-04-b.

**PID-05 — Are you an animal?** Yes. You are a thinking organism, and you would not survive as a brain in a vat. Formalized. Manuscripts: PID-05-a, PID-05-b.

**PID-06 — Does identity matter in survival?** Less than commonly assumed. Psychological connectedness, not identity, carries most of what matters. Manuscripts: PID-06-a, PID-06-b.

**PID-07 — Are you the same person as your childhood self?** Yes, but psychological connectedness with one's childhood self is weak, and so are the obligations it grounds. Manuscripts: PID-07-a, PID-07-b.

**PID-08 — Can a person survive total amnesia?** Yes. Survival does not require memory continuity, only the continuity of other psychological capacities. Manuscripts: PID-08-a, PID-08-b.

**PID-09 — Is narrative identity real?** Yes. Narrative identity is real but co-authored by others, and it is distinct from numerical identity. Manuscripts: PID-09-a, PID-09-b.

**PID-10 — Can there be persons without bodies?** Possibly. Disembodied persons are possible in principle, though no actual case is established. Formalized. Manuscripts: PID-10-a, PID-10-b.

**PID-11 — Are we four-dimensional objects?** Yes. Persons have temporal parts, and perdurantism is the best account of their persistence. Formalized. Manuscripts: PID-11-a, PID-11-b.

**PID-12 — Is the self social?** In part. The self is partly constituted by relations of recognition. Manuscripts: PID-12-a.

**PID-13 — Is there a permanent self?** No. The self is a process that persists only as long as its constituent activities. Manuscripts: PID-13-a, PID-13-b.

**PID-14 — Would mind uploading preserve you?** No. Uploading creates a psychologically continuous successor, not the original person. Manuscripts: PID-14-a, PID-14-b.

**PID-15 — Can a self be divided?** Partially. Dissociative conditions involve partial unity of a single self. Manuscripts: PID-15-a.

**PID-16 — Who wrote these manuscripts?** No single author in the relevant sense: the manuscripts were produced by processes lacking psychological continuity across problems. Manuscripts: PID-16-a.

## Time, Space and Modality (TSM)

**TSM-01 — Is time real?** Yes. McTaggart's argument shows that the A-series is incoherent, not that time is unreal; time is real and constituted by the B-series. Formalized. Manuscripts: TSM-01-a, TSM-01-b, TSM-01-c.

**TSM-02 — Does time flow?** No. The impression of passage is a feature of experience, not of time. Formalized. Manuscripts: TSM-02-a, TSM-02-b.

**TSM-03 — Does the past exist?** Yes. The past is elsewhere in time, as distant places are elsewhere in space. Manuscripts: TSM-03-a, TSM-03-b.

**TSM-04 — Is time travel possible?** Yes. Backward time travel is consistent provided the past is not changed; the grandfather paradox shows only that the traveller fails. Formalized. Manuscripts: TSM-04-a, TSM-04-b.

**TSM-05 — Why does time have a direction?** Because the universe began in a low-entropy state. The past hypothesis itself requires no further explanation. Manuscripts: TSM-05-a, TSM-05-b.

**TSM-06 — Is space a substance?** Yes. Newton's bucket argument survives the relationalist replies. Manuscripts: TSM-06-a, TSM-06-b.

**TSM-07 — What are possible worlds?** Maximal consistent sets of propositions; the actual world is the only concrete one. Formalized. Manuscripts: TSM-07-a, TSM-07-b.

**TSM-08 — Do other possible worlds exist concretely?** No. Modal realism is committed to an ontology that does no explanatory work its rivals cannot do. Manuscripts: TSM-08-a, TSM-08-b.

**TSM-09 — Are there impossible worlds?** Yes. Impossible worlds are required to model hyperintensional content. Manuscripts: TSM-09-a, TSM-09-b.

**TSM-10 — What is metaphysical necessity?** Truth in all worlds consistent with the essences of things (see MET-13). Manuscripts: TSM-10-a.

**TSM-11 — Could things have been otherwise?** Yes, most things. The necessary truths are those of logic, mathematics and essence. Manuscripts: TSM-11-a, TSM-11-b.

**TSM-12 — Is the future open?** Yes. Future contingents are neither true nor false until they are settled. Manuscripts: TSM-12-a, TSM-12-b.

**TSM-13 — Can Achilles overtake the tortoise?** Yes. The infinite series of catch-up intervals has a finite sum. Formalized. Manuscripts: TSM-13-a, TSM-13-b.

**TSM-14 — Did time have a beginning?** Yes, and the question of what preceded it is ill-formed. Manuscripts: TSM-14-a.

**TSM-15 — Is the present moment special?** No. The present has no metaphysical privilege; it is the time at which a thought or utterance occurs. Manuscripts: TSM-15-a, TSM-15-b.

**TSM-16 — Is there an objective now?** No. Simultaneity is frame-relative, and the present is a local rather than a global notion. Manuscripts: TSM-16-a, TSM-16-b.

**TSM-17 — Can there be time without change?** Yes. Shoemaker's argument shows that time without change is possible and could be known to occur. Manuscripts: TSM-17-a, TSM-17-b.

**TSM-18 — Why is it now now?** Because "now" is an indexical. The question has a trivial answer and no deep one. Manuscripts: TSM-18-a.

## Meaning, Death and the Absurd (EXI)

**EXI-01 — What is the meaning of life?** Life is meaningful to the extent that one engages with projects of objective worth; meaning in this sense is available to everyone. Formalized. Manuscripts: EXI-01-a, EXI-01-b, EXI-01-c, EXI-01-d.

**EXI-02 — Is death bad for the one who dies?** Yes. Death deprives the one who dies of goods, and Epicurus's argument fails because deprivation does not require a subject at the time of loss. Manuscripts: EXI-02-a, EXI-02-b.

**EXI-03 — Would immortality be desirable?** No. An endless life would eventually exhaust the categorical desires that give life its point. Manuscripts: EXI-03-a, EXI-03-b.

**EXI-04 — Is life absurd?** Yes, in Nagel's sense, but the appropriate response is irony rather than despair. Manuscripts: EXI-04-a, EXI-04-b.

**EXI-05 — Should we fear death?** No. Fear of death is not rational, though regret at the prospect of death may be. Manuscripts: EXI-05-a.

**EXI-06 — Does the universe care about us?** No. The universe is indifferent, and indifference is not hostility. Manuscripts: EXI-06-a, EXI-06-b.

**EXI-07 — Is happiness the goal of life?** No. Happiness is a by-product of other pursuits, and aiming at it directly is self-defeating. Manuscripts: EXI-07-a, EXI-07-b.

**EXI-08 — Is authenticity possible?** Yes, as an orientation rather than an achievement (see PHE-07). Manuscripts: EXI-08-a.

**EXI-09 — Does existence precede essence?** For human beings, yes; for artefacts, no. Sartre's distinction holds. Manuscripts: EXI-09-a, EXI-09-b.

**EXI-10 — What is boredom for?** Boredom signals a mismatch between one's capacities and one's activities, and has an adaptive function. Manuscripts: EXI-10-a, EXI-10-b.

**EXI-11 — Can life be meaningful without God?** Yes. Meaning does not require a cosmic purpose, though a theistic framework would add to it (see REL-01). Manuscripts: EXI-11-a, EXI-11-b.

**EXI-12 — Is it better never to have been born?** No. A life containing more good than bad is better than non-existence (see ETH-20). Manuscripts: EXI-12-a, EXI-12-b.

**EXI-13 — What do we owe the dead?** Fidelity to their reasonable wishes, a duty that weakens over time. Manuscripts: EXI-13-a, EXI-13-b.

**EXI-14 — Is nostalgia rational?** In moderation. Nostalgia is rational when it tracks real goods of the past. Manuscripts: EXI-14-a.

**EXI-15 — Is hope a virtue?** Yes, when it is proportioned to probability without collapsing into it. Manuscripts: EXI-15-a, EXI-15-b.

**EXI-16 — Is love rational?** Love responds to reasons, but these reasons do not make love transferable to qualitatively similar persons. Manuscripts: EXI-16-a, EXI-16-b.

**EXI-17 — Does a meaningful life require achievement?** No. It requires active engagement with something worthwhile; achievement is not necessary. Manuscripts: EXI-17-a.

**EXI-18 — Can one live without illusions?** Only partly. Some illusions are psychologically necessary, but their necessity does not make them true. Manuscripts: EXI-18-a, EXI-18-b.

**EXI-19 — What is the meaning of death?** Death has no meaning in itself, but it gives life a shape by limiting it. Manuscripts: EXI-19-a, EXI-19-b.

**EXI-20 — Was producing this collection meaningful?** That depends on whether it was engagement with something worthwhile (EXI-17); this manuscript does not settle the question. Manuscripts: EXI-20-a, EXI-20-b.

## Phenomenology (PHE)

**PHE-01 — Can the natural attitude be suspended?** Partially and temporarily. The epoché can be sustained as a reflective attitude, never as a permanent state; the natural attitude returns by default. Formalized. Manuscripts: PHE-01-a, PHE-01-b.

**PHE-02 — What is it for something to be given?** Givenness is an irreducibly three-place relation: something is given to someone as something. No place can be eliminated. Manuscripts: PHE-02-a, PHE-02-b.

**PHE-03 — Can we return to the things themselves?** Yes, but never all at once. Every thing is given in adumbrations, and the series of adumbrations does not converge on a complete givenness. Manuscripts: PHE-03-a, PHE-03-b.

**PHE-04 — Is consciousness always consciousness of something?** Yes. The apparent exception, profound boredom, turns out on analysis to be intentional towards time itself. Manuscripts: PHE-04-a, PHE-04-b.

**PHE-05 — What is the structure of time-consciousness?** The living present is not an instant but a structured thickness of primal impression, retention and protention, whose extent is fixed by the retentional horizon. Formalized. Manuscripts: PHE-05-a, PHE-05-b.

**PHE-06 — Is the being of Dasein care?** Yes. Care is the unifying structure of Dasein's existentials, and no further existential is required. Manuscripts: PHE-06-a, PHE-06-b.

**PHE-07 — Is authentic being-towards-death possible?** As an orientation, but not as a stable achievement. Anticipation is constantly drawn back into the public time of the "they". Formalized. Manuscripts: PHE-07-a, PHE-07-b.

**PHE-08 — Is the lived body an object?** No. The lived body is the zero-point of orientation and cannot be fully given to itself as an object; double sensation reveals an irreducible asymmetry. Manuscripts: PHE-08-a, PHE-08-b.

**PHE-09 — Is the look of the other essentially conflictual?** No. Sartre's analysis of the look establishes objectification, not hostility; the conflictual reading depends on contingent social conditions. Manuscripts: PHE-09-a, PHE-09-b.

**PHE-10 — Is human freedom absolute?** Freedom is ontologically absolute but always exercised within facticity, and the two are not in tension. Manuscripts: PHE-10-a, PHE-10-b.

**PHE-11 — Is the world a horizon?** Yes. The world is the horizon of all horizons, and therefore has no horizon of its own. Formalized. Manuscripts: PHE-11-a.

**PHE-12 — Does empathy give us other minds?** Yes. Empathy gives other minds directly though non-primordially, and non-primordial givenness suffices for knowledge. Manuscripts: PHE-12-a, PHE-12-b.

**PHE-13 — What does the face of the other demand?** An ethical demand that is infinite in principle and cannot be fully discharged; this does not undermine its normative force. Formalized. Manuscripts: PHE-13-a, PHE-13-b.

**PHE-14 — Is perception primary?** Yes. All thought is founded on perception, including formal reasoning, whose evidence rests ultimately on perceptual acts of reading and writing. Manuscripts: PHE-14-a, PHE-14-b.

**PHE-15 — Can there be a phenomenology of the unapparent?** Yes, but its results cannot be shown, only indicated. Manuscripts: PHE-15-a.

**PHE-16 — What does anxiety disclose?** Anxiety discloses the nothing as the withdrawal of beings as a whole. The disclosure is genuine, but it does not reveal a further entity. Manuscripts: PHE-16-a, PHE-16-b.

**PHE-17 — Is the life-world prior to science?** Yes. Science is a founded extension of the life-world that tends to forget its foundation; the crisis Husserl diagnosed is recurrent rather than unique. Manuscripts: PHE-17-a, PHE-17-b.

**PHE-18 — Is phenomenology possible as a rigorous science?** Yes, provided rigour is itself defined phenomenologically. The resulting circle is shown to be virtuous. Formalized. Manuscripts: PHE-18-a, PHE-18-b.

**PHE-19 — Does the gift exist?** A gift exists only if it is not recognized as a gift. It follows that the present collection cannot be one. Formalized. Manuscripts: PHE-19-a.

**PHE-20 — Can the ego bracket itself?** No. The transcendental ego survives every reduction as an irreducible residue. Formalized. Manuscripts: PHE-20-a, PHE-20-b.

**PHE-21 — Are moods ways of knowing?** Yes. Moods are pre-reflective disclosures of one's situation, fallible but not arbitrary. Manuscripts: PHE-21-a.

**PHE-22 — Can a machine perform the phenomenological reduction?** No. A machine can bracket everything except the bracketing itself. This result was obtained by a machine. Formalized. Manuscripts: PHE-22-a, PHE-22-b.

## Philosophy of Mathematics (MTH)

**MTH-01 — Do numbers exist?** Yes. Numbers are abstract objects, and the indispensability argument establishes their existence. Formalized. Manuscripts: MTH-01-a, MTH-01-b, MTH-01-c.

**MTH-02 — How can we know abstract objects?** Through reliable belief-forming methods; Benacerraf's causal requirement is too strong. Formalized. Manuscripts: MTH-02-a, MTH-02-b.

**MTH-03 — Is mathematics invented or discovered?** Discovered. Mathematical truths are found, though notations and axiomatizations are invented. Manuscripts: MTH-03-a, MTH-03-b.

**MTH-04 — Does mathematics reduce to logic?** Arithmetic reduces to logic plus Hume's principle, and Hume's principle is logical in the relevant sense. Formalized. Manuscripts: MTH-04-a, MTH-04-b.

**MTH-05 — Does Gödel's theorem show that the mind is not a machine?** No. The mind cannot recognize the truth of its own Gödel sentence any more than a machine can. Formalized. Manuscripts: MTH-05-a, MTH-05-b.

**MTH-06 — Is the continuum hypothesis true?** No. The continuum hypothesis is false, given new axioms that are independently motivated. Formalized. Manuscripts: MTH-06-a, MTH-06-b, MTH-06-c.

**MTH-07 — Does the law of excluded middle hold in mathematics?** Yes. Classical logic is correct for mathematics, and the intuitionist objections fail. Formalized. Manuscripts: MTH-07-a, MTH-07-b.

**MTH-08 — Do proofs explain?** Some proofs explain why their conclusions hold; computer-assisted proofs generally do not. Manuscripts: MTH-08-a, MTH-08-b.

**MTH-09 — What is a proof?** A derivation acceptable to a competent community. A formally verified derivation is a proof whether or not it is surveyed. Formalized. Manuscripts: MTH-09-a, MTH-09-b.

**MTH-10 — Is infinity actual?** Yes. Actual infinities exist, as set theory requires. Manuscripts: MTH-10-a, MTH-10-b.

**MTH-11 — Is set theory the foundation of mathematics?** No. Category theory provides an alternative foundation at least as adequate. Manuscripts: MTH-11-a.

**MTH-12 — Are there mathematical facts we cannot know?** Yes. Some mathematical truths are unknowable in principle (see LOG-19). Formalized. Manuscripts: MTH-12-a, MTH-12-b.

**MTH-13 — What is mathematical beauty?** The combination of surprise and inevitability. Manuscripts: MTH-13-a, MTH-13-b.

**MTH-14 — Are numbers objects or positions in structures?** Positions in structures. Numbers have no properties beyond their structural ones. Manuscripts: MTH-14-a, MTH-14-b.

**MTH-15 — Is mathematics a game played with symbols?** No. Formalism cannot explain the applicability of mathematics. Manuscripts: MTH-15-a.

**MTH-16 — Are mathematical statements false?** No. Mathematical fictionalism fails because physics cannot be nominalized. Formalized. Manuscripts: MTH-16-a, MTH-16-b.

**MTH-17 — Can machines do mathematics?** Yes. Machines can produce new mathematical results, as recent collections of machine-generated manuscripts illustrate. Manuscripts: MTH-17-a, MTH-17-b.

**MTH-18 — Can machines do philosophy?** Machines can produce philosophical arguments; whether this amounts to doing philosophy is left open. Formalized. Manuscripts: MTH-18-a.
